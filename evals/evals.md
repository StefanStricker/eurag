> The GDPR provides several legal bases for processing personal data, as outlined in Article 6(1):
>

> - Performance of a contract (Article 6(1)(b)).

> - Protection of the vital interests of the data subject or another natural person (Article 6(1)(d)).

<details><summary><code>aiact-01</code> Which AI practices are prohibited under the AI Act?</summary>

> The AI Act prohibits the following AI practices:
>
> - AI systems that deploy subliminal techniques or manipulative or deceptive techniques, with the objective or effect of materially distorting the behavior of a person or a group of persons, thereby causing or being reasonably likely to cause significant harm [1][2].
>
> - AI systems that exploit the vulnerabilities of a natural person or a specific group of persons due to their age, disability, or a specific social or economic situation, with the objective or effect of materially distorting the behavior of that person or a person belonging to that group, causing or being reasonably likely to cause significant harm [1][2].
>
> - AI systems used for evaluating or classifying natural persons or groups of persons over a certain period based on their social behavior or known, inferred, or predicted personal or personality characteristics, leading to either or both of the following:
>   - Detrimental or unfavorable treatment of certain natural persons or groups of persons in social contexts unrelated to the contexts in which the data was originally generated or collected [1][2].
>   - Detrimental or unfavorable treatment of certain natural persons or groups of persons that is unjustified or disproportionate to their social behavior or its gravity [1][2].
>
> - AI systems used for making risk assessments of natural persons to assess or predict the risk of a natural person committing a criminal offense, based solely on the profiling of a natural person or on assessing their personality traits and characteristics, unless used to support human assessment based on objective and verifiable facts [1][2].
>
> - AI systems used to create or expand facial recognition databases through untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage [1][2].
>
> - AI systems used to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where intended for medical or safety reasons [1][2].
>
> - Biometric categorization systems that categorize individuals based on their biometric data to deduce or infer their race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life, or sexual orientation [1][2].
>
> - Real-time remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement purposes, unless strictly necessary for specific objectives such as searching for victims, preventing imminent threats, or identifying persons suspected of criminal offenses [1][5][6][7][8].

<details><summary><code>aiact-03</code> When is an AI system classified as high-risk?</summary>

> An AI system is classified as high-risk if it meets the conditions outlined in Article 6(1) and (2) of the AI Act. Specifically:
>
> - It is intended to be used as a safety component of a product, or it is itself a product, covered by Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I [1].
> - The product whose safety component is the AI system, or the AI system itself, is required to undergo a third-party conformity assessment [1].
>
> Additionally, AI systems listed in Annex III are automatically considered high-risk [2]. However, an AI system in Annex III is not considered high-risk if it does not pose a significant risk of harm to health, safety, or fundamental rights of natural persons, under certain conditions [2]. These conditions include performing narrow procedural tasks, improving previous human activities, detecting decision-making patterns without influencing outcomes, or performing preparatory tasks for assessments relevant to use cases in Annex III [2].
>
> Furthermore, the AI system must be used in a number of specifically pre-defined areas as specified in the Regulation [4].

<details><summary><code>aiact-05</code> What does the AI Act require regarding human oversight?</summary>

> The AI Act requires human oversight for high-risk AI systems to ensure their safe and ethical use. Specifically:
>
> - Human oversight measures must be commensurate with the risks, level of autonomy, and context of use of the high-risk AI system [1][2][3][4][6].
>
> - Providers must ensure that natural persons assigned human oversight can understand the system's capacities and limitations, monitor its operation, and intervene if necessary [2][3].
>
> - For certain high-risk AI systems, particularly those used in law enforcement, migration, border control, and asylum, no action or decision may be taken based on the system's identification unless separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons with the necessary competence, training, and authority [4][5][7][8].
>
> - Deployers must assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training, and authority, and ensure they use the systems in accordance with the instructions for use [6].
>
> - Market surveillance authorities, particularly for high-risk AI systems used in law enforcement, border management, and justice and democracy, must be designated to monitor and evaluate AI systems for compliance with the AI Act [7][8].

<details><summary><code>aiact-06</code> What transparency obligations apply to chatbots and deepfakes?</summary>

> - **Chatbots**: Providers of AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons must ensure that the natural persons are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the point of view of a reasonably well-informed, observant, and circumspect person, considering the circumstances and context of use [1][3][8].

Additionally, the disclosure must be provided in a clear and distinguishable manner at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure, and it should conform to accessibility requirements [1][2][3][8].

<details><summary><code>aiact-08</code> When is a general-purpose AI model considered to have systemic risk?</summary>

<details><summary><code>dataact-04</code> When are contractual terms on data use considered unfair?</summary>

> Contractual terms on data use are considered unfair if they:
>
> - Grossly deviate from good commercial practice in data access and use, contrary to good faith and fair dealing [1][5].
> - Exclude or limit the liability of the party that unilaterally imposed the term for intentional acts or gross negligence, or exclude the remedies available to the other party upon non-performance of contractual obligations [1][2].
> - Give the party that unilaterally imposed the term the exclusive right to determine data conformity or interpretation [1].
> - Inappropriately limit remedies in the case of non-performance or liability breaches, or extend the liability of the other party [2].
> - Allow the party that unilaterally imposed the term to access and use data in a manner significantly detrimental to the other party's legitimate interests, especially if the data are commercially sensitive or protected by trade secrets or intellectual property rights [2].
> - Prevent the other party from using data provided or generated during the contract period or limit such use [2].
> - Prevent the other party from terminating the agreement within a reasonable period [2].
> - Prevent the other party from obtaining a copy of the data provided or generated during the contract period or within a reasonable period after its termination [2].
> - Enable the party that unilaterally imposed the term to terminate the contract at unreasonably short notice, considering the other party's ability to switch to an alternative service and financial detriment [2].
> - Enable the party that unilaterally imposed the term to substantially change the price or other substantive conditions related to data sharing without a valid reason or right to terminate the contract [2].
>
> These terms are presumed unfair if they:
>
> - Inappropriately limit remedies or extend liability [2].
> - Allow access to data in a manner significantly detrimental to the other party's legitimate interests [2].
> - Prevent the other party from using data or limit such use [2].
> - Prevent the other party from terminating the agreement within a reasonable period [2].
> - Prevent the other party from obtaining a copy of the data [2].
> - Enable the party to terminate the contract at unreasonably short notice [2].
> - Enable the party to substantially change the price or other substantive conditions without a valid reason [2].
>
> The rules apply specifically to terms concerning access to and use of data, liability, and remedies for breach or termination of data-related obligations, and only if they are unilaterally imposed by one party on another [1][6].

<details><summary><code>dataact-08</code> What safeguards apply against unlawful third-country government access to non-personal data?</summary>

<details><summary><code>dga-02</code> What is a data intermediation service?</summary>

> [1][3] A data intermediation service is a service that aims to establish commercial relationships for the purposes of data sharing between an undetermined number of data subjects and data holders on the one hand and data users on the other, through technical, legal or other means. However, it excludes services that aggregate, enrich, or transform data for substantial value addition or those used in closed groups.

<details><summary><code>xdoc-03</code> How does the GDPR right to data portability differ from the Data Act’s data access right?</summary>

> - The GDPR right to data portability [1][5] applies specifically to personal data provided by the data subject, whereas the Data Act’s data access right [2][8] covers both personal and non-personal data, including product data or related service data.
> - Under GDPR, the right to data portability is limited to data processed by automated means on the basis of consent or a contract [1][4]. The Data Act, however, mandates the provision of data to users without undue delay, regardless of the legal basis for processing [2][8].
> - GDPR allows for the direct transmission of data from one controller to another where technically feasible [1][3]. The Data Act also supports direct transmission but emphasizes the technical feasibility and the right of users to make data available to third parties [2][8][9].
> - GDPR’s right to data portability does not apply where processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority [1][3]. The Data Act, on the other hand, complements GDPR by ensuring the technical feasibility of data access for all types of data, whether personal or non-personal, and by allowing users to seek remedies if data holders refuse to share data [2][8][9].
> - GDPR requires data controllers to inform data subjects about their rights, including the right to data portability, at the time of data collection [6][7]. The Data Act does not explicitly mention such an obligation but emphasizes the user's right to access and make data available to third parties [2][8].

<details><summary><code>xdoc-06</code> What rules on data access by public bodies exist in the DGA and the Data Act, and how do they differ?</summary>

> - **Data Governance Act (DGA):**
>   - **Article 21:** Public sector bodies, the Commission, the European Central Bank, and Union bodies can share data with research organizations or statistical bodies for scientific research or official statistics production. [8]
>   - **Article 19:** Obligations include not using data in a manner incompatible with the purpose for which they were requested, implementing security measures, and erasing data once no longer necessary. [9]
>
> - **Data Act:**
>   - **Article 17:** Public sector bodies, the Commission, the European Central Bank, and Union bodies cannot make data available for reuse as defined in other regulations. They can exchange data with other public bodies or make it available to third parties under certain conditions. [5]
>   - **Article 21:** Similar to the DGA, allows sharing data with research organizations or statistical bodies, but with additional conditions on the use of the data. [8]
>
> - **Differences:**
>   - The DGA focuses more on the re-use of data by public sector bodies, while the Data Act emphasizes the sharing of data between public bodies and third parties. [6][8][5]
>   - The DGA allows for the re-use of data for scientific research and official statistics, whereas the Data Act restricts the reuse of data as defined in other regulations. [8][5]
